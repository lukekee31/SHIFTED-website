V Engine

Compact, powerful and built for performance. The V engine allows manufacturers to fit more cylinders into a shorter engine, making it the preferred choice for many sports cars and performance vehicles.

Overview

A V engine arranges its cylinders into two separate banks that meet at an angle, forming the shape of the letter "V". This arrangement allows manufacturers to install engines with six, eight, ten or even twelve cylinders while keeping the engine shorter than an equivalent inline engine.

Because of this compact design, V engines are commonly used in sports cars, luxury sedans and high-performance SUVs where greater power is required.

How a V Engine is Arranged

Instead of arranging every cylinder in a straight line, V engines split the cylinders into two separate banks connected to a common crankshaft. This design shortens the engine while allowing more cylinders to be fitted.

Advantages

More Power

Supports higher cylinder counts, ideal for performance vehicles.

Compact Length

Shorter than an equivalent inline engine, allowing better packaging.

Smooth Operation

V6, V8 and V12 engines provide smoother power delivery than smaller engines.

Performance

Excellent for sports cars, luxury vehicles and racing.

Disadvantages

More Complex

Contains more components than many inline engines.

Higher Manufacturing Cost

More expensive to produce.

More Difficult Maintenance

The two cylinder banks reduce accessibility.

Increased Weight

Typically heavier than equivalent inline engines.

Quick Facts

  • Common V angles include 60°, 90° and 72°.
  • V8 engines are widely used in performance cars.
  • V12 engines are among the smoothest engine layouts.
  • Shorter than comparable inline engines.
