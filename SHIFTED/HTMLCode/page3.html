Engine Power

Discover how internal combustion engines convert fuel into motion and explore the different engine layouts used by manufacturers around the world.

Turning Fuel Into Motion

Internal combustion engines have powered automobiles for over a century. Although modern engines differ in size and performance, they all operate using the same basic principle: converting the chemical energy stored in fuel into mechanical energy that turns the wheels.

Understanding this process makes it easier to appreciate why different engine layouts exist and why manufacturers choose one design over another.

How an Engine Works

Air enters the cylinder, fuel is injected, and a spark ignites the mixture. The expanding gases push the piston downward, and the crankshaft converts that piston movement into the rotational motion that drives the wheels.

The Four-Stroke Cycle

Almost every modern petrol engine repeats this same four-step cycle thousands of times per minute.

1. Intake

The intake valve opens while the piston moves downward, allowing air and fuel to enter the cylinder.

2. Compression

The piston moves upward, compressing the air-fuel mixture to prepare for combustion.

3. Power

The spark plug ignites the compressed mixture, forcing the piston downward and producing useful power.

4. Exhaust

The exhaust valve opens while the piston moves upward, pushing burnt gases out before the cycle begins again.

Engine Components

Select each component to learn more about the role it plays.

Piston

Converts combustion pressure into linear movement.

The piston is pushed down by expanding gases during combustion, transferring that force to the connecting rod.

Connecting Rod

Transfers force from the piston to the crankshaft.

This rigid link connects the piston to the crankshaft, converting linear force into rotational input.

Crankshaft

Converts linear piston motion into rotational motion.

The crankshaft turns the up-and-down motion of the pistons into the spinning motion that ultimately drives the wheels.

Cylinder

The chamber where combustion occurs.

Each cylinder houses a piston and is where the air-fuel mixture is compressed and ignited.

Spark Plug

Ignites the compressed air-fuel mixture in petrol engines.

A precisely timed electrical spark ignites the mixture at the top of the compression stroke.

Valves

Control the intake of air-fuel mixture and the release of exhaust gases.

Intake and exhaust valves open and close in sync with the piston to manage airflow in and out of the cylinder.

Choose an Engine Layout

All three layouts use the same four-stroke cycle, but their cylinder arrangement affects packaging, balance, performance and manufacturing cost.

Inline Engine

Inline engines arrange all cylinders in a single straight line. They are simple, compact and economical, making them the most common engine layout used in everyday passenger vehicles.

V Engine

V engines position two banks of cylinders in a V shape. This design allows more cylinders to fit into a shorter engine while delivering higher performance.

Boxer Engine

Boxer engines position cylinders horizontally opposite each other. Their low centre of gravity improves handling and vehicle stability.

Summary

Although every internal combustion engine follows the same four-stroke cycle, manufacturers choose different engine layouts depending on their priorities. Inline engines prioritise simplicity and affordability, V engines deliver greater performance in a compact package, while Boxer engines offer exceptional balance and a lower centre of gravity.
