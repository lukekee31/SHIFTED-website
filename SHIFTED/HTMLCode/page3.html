Engine Power

Internal combustion engines have powered cars for over a century. Learn how they work, why different layouts exist and what makes each engine unique.

The Basics

What an Engine Actually Does

At its core, an engine converts the energy stored in fuel into motion. Small, controlled explosions inside the cylinders push pistons up and down, and that movement is ultimately turned into the rotation that drives the wheels.

Manufacturers arrange cylinders differently — in a line, a V shape, or opposite each other — to balance size, smoothness, cost and performance for different types of vehicles.

The Four-Stroke Cycle

How a Petrol Engine Breathes

Intake

The piston moves down, drawing an air-fuel mixture into the cylinder.

Compression

The piston rises, compressing the mixture to prepare it for ignition.

Power

A spark ignites the mixture, forcing the piston down with power.

Exhaust

The piston rises again, pushing spent gases out of the cylinder.

Engine Layout Explorer

Inline Engine

Manufacturers: Toyota, Honda, BMW

Example cars: Corolla, Civic, Mazda 3

Advantages: Compact, low cost, simple to manufacture.

Disadvantages: Can feel less smooth at higher cylinder counts.

Interesting fact: The inline layout is the most common configuration in everyday passenger cars.

V Engine

Manufacturers: Ferrari, Mercedes-AMG, Ford

Example cars: Mustang GT, Ferrari F8, AMG GT

Advantages: Very smooth, allows higher cylinder counts in a shorter space.

Disadvantages: Wider, heavier and more expensive to produce.

Interesting fact: V engines are favoured in high-performance and luxury vehicles for their smoothness.

Boxer Engine

Manufacturers: Subaru, Porsche

Example cars: WRX, BRZ / GR86, Porsche 911

Advantages: Very low centre of gravity, excellent balance.

Disadvantages: Wider engine bay, more costly to service.

Interesting fact: Horizontally opposed cylinders "box" against each other, giving the engine its name.
