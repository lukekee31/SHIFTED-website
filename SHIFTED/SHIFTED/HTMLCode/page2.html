Part 01 · Racing & Innovation

Motorsport Technology

Racing has always pushed engineering to its limits. Many technologies developed for competition eventually improve the safety, performance and reliability of the cars we drive every day.

Why Motorsport?

A Laboratory on Wheels

Motorsport acts as a laboratory where engineers are constantly searching for a competitive edge. Under intense pressure and tight timelines, teams develop technologies that would take years to reach production cars through ordinary research.

Many of these innovations — from braking systems to aerodynamics — are later refined and adopted by manufacturers, eventually becoming standard features in the cars we drive on public roads.

Racing Innovations Explained

How it works: ABS rapidly pulses the brakes to prevent the wheels from locking up, allowing the driver to maintain steering control while braking hard.

Motorsport example: Early anti-lock systems were tested in racing and rallying, where consistent braking performance under extreme conditions was critical.

Road car example: ABS is now a standard safety feature on virtually every new car sold today.

How it works: Traction control detects wheel spin and reduces engine power or applies braking to individual wheels to maintain grip.

Motorsport example: Formula One pioneered sophisticated traction control systems in the 1990s to maximise corner-exit speed.

Road car example: Nearly all modern cars use traction control to keep drivers safe in wet or slippery conditions.

How it works: Active suspension uses sensors and actuators to continuously adjust ride height and damping for optimal handling.

Motorsport example: The Williams FW14B and FW15C Formula One cars famously used active suspension to dominate their era.

Road car example: Adaptive suspension systems are now available on many performance and luxury vehicles.

How it works: Electronically controlled gearboxes allow near-instant gear changes without a traditional clutch pedal.

Motorsport example: Ferrari introduced paddle-shift semi-automatic gearboxes to Formula One in 1989.

Road car example: Dual-clutch and paddle-shift transmissions are now common in performance road cars.

Uses electronic stability systems refined through decades of motorsport.

Advanced all-wheel-drive electronics developed through racing programmes.

Mercedes-AMG GT

Benefits from performance braking technology proven in GT racing.

Its AWD system was developed directly from Toyota's rally programme.

Did you know? The Williams FW15C is widely regarded as one of the most technologically advanced Formula One cars ever built, featuring active suspension, traction control and semi-automatic gear shifting.

The Track Never Stops Teaching

Motorsport continues to improve everyday cars. Technologies first proven on the track eventually benefit millions of drivers, and innovation continues through modern racing series such as Formula One, the FIA World Endurance Championship and Formula E.
